Synthesis of PMC application rejection reasons and editorial requirements for journals

1. Purpose and scope
This document synthesizes the decision letters received from the National Library of Medicine (NLM) by 10 journals whose applications to PubMed Central (PMC) were unsuccessful. It (1) classifies the reasons for rejection and (2) derives the requirements and practical checklists that journals should apply during editing and publication.
Nine of the letters were scientific quality review outcomes that itemized specific concerns in sampled articles. One was closed at the initial application screening stage because of "significant concerns over the scientific and editorial quality"; for that journal, NLM stated it would want to see "evidence of more rigorous evaluation methods and improved clarity of the reporting across articles."
A journal rejected at screening or scientific quality review is eligible to reapply after 24 months, so that interval should be used to make structural changes to the editorial process rather than cosmetic ones.

2.1 Study design, execution, and interpretation (8/9)
This is the most fundamental and most widely shared concern. The recurring formulations were:
•	Study aims and design choices for which adequate rationale was not provided
•	Study aims not clearly stated in the introduction
•	Study designs that were not robust — structurally inadequate for the question posed
•	Conclusions that were overstated, over-generalized, not supported by the results, or not aligned with the stated aim
•	Errors in statistical reporting, including p values reported as "0.000" instead of p < 0.001
NLM defines scientific rigor as "the strict application of the scientific method to ensure robust and unbiased experimental design, methodology, analysis, interpretation and reporting of results." A concern in this category is therefore read as evidence about the journal's review capacity, not merely about one article.
2.2 Reproducibility of the methods (8/9)
The sentence "methods that were not described clearly and in sufficient detail to enable reproducibility" appeared in almost every letter. It is the most mechanically verifiable concern, and the one most readily controlled at the editorial stage. Methods sections should specify:
•	Eligibility and exclusion criteria, sample size justification, recruitment period and setting
•	Source, validity, and reliability of instruments; timing and procedure of measurement
•	Content, dose, duration, and provider of any intervention
•	Statistical methods, software and version, significance level, and handling of missing data
•	Ethics committee, approval number, and the method of obtaining consent
2.3 Overstated conclusions and unsupported statements (6/9, 5/9)
Overstatement travels with citation failure. The citation concerns fell into three levels:
1.	Statements made with no citation provided
2.	Citations provided that did not support the assertion made
3.	Citations that were outdated
The quality of the reference list itself was also criticized. In one journal, an article relied heavily on master's theses and dissertations, many of which were inaccessible because of non-functioning links; in another article, reference entries were incomplete or contained errors.
2.4 Editorial quality, tables, and figures (7/9, 5/9)
•	Content difficult to read and understand because of problems with writing clarity — grammar, diction, typos, logical flow
•	Unnecessary abbreviations, for example terms defined and then never used again
•	Data repeated between text and tables or figures
•	Typos in tables and figures, lack of annotation and informative legends, poor construction
•	Tables and figures cited in the text that did not show the indicated data
Editorial quality is the first signal a reviewer registers on opening an article, and it shapes the impression formed of the journal's overall editorial management.
2.5 Requirements specific to article types (3/9, 2/9)
•	Reviews: repeatedly judged not critical and comprehensive; in one case a review was devoted largely to describing the authors' previous work rather than summarizing and analyzing recent research in the field.
•	Systematic reviews: did not adequately adhere to PRISMA guidelines.
•	Case reports: did not adequately adhere to CARE guidelines.
•	Discussion sections: not critical and comprehensive, and in several journals did not fully address limitations.
•	Abstracts: did not provide sufficient detail to describe the content of the article, in line with ICMJE Recommendations.
2.6 Structural concerns at the journal level
These concerns address editorial policy and journal operation rather than individual manuscripts, and cannot be resolved by strengthening manuscript review alone.
Area	What the letters stated
Article type mix	Relatively few original research articles in recent issues compared with other article types such as reviews; despite a stated aim of publishing research, most recent content consisted of other types such as case reports
In-house authorship	While the journal had lowered the proportion of primary articles authored in-house, many articles were still authored by its editors and editorial board members
Currency of data	An article in which the data were more than 5 years old at the time of publication, and another in which the samples were more than 15 years old
Internal consistency	An article that stated it was a prospective study but declared in the ethics statement that it was retrospective
Consistency of quality	"Article quality is variable / highly variable"; "a few articles were of fair or good quality"; "occasional articles were of good quality" — consistency, not the existence of good articles, is what is assessed
The last row deserves emphasis. NLM does not ask how many strong articles a journal has published; it asks whether quality is sustained across what is published. A small number of weak articles determines the outcome for the journal as a whole.
3. What a journal must put in place before applying to PMC
3.1 Eligibility requirements
•	An ISSN properly registered through the ISSN International Centre
•	The ability to give NLM immediate access to journal content
•	At least 25 peer-reviewed articles already published
•	Compliance with PMC's language requirements
•	At least a two-year history of quality scholarly publishing in the life sciences, with exceptions possible for newer publishers under experienced management
3.2 Editorial policy and governance
•	Publish transparent aims and scope, an explicit peer review process, conflict-of-interest disclosure, and research ethics standards on the journal website
•	List editors with names, institutional affiliations, and areas of expertise; ensure the board reflects the journal scope and shows diversity in both editors and authors
•	Document and apply a defined conflict-of-interest procedure for manuscripts authored by editors or board members — independent handling editor, firewalled review, and disclosure in the published article
•	Set and monitor an internal ceiling on the annual proportion of articles authored by editors and board members
•	Adhere to the stated publication schedule and maintain a sustainable publication volume
•	Set a target proportion for original research and ensure the actual mix of article types matches the journal's stated aims
3.3 Strengthening peer review
•	Adopt a structured review form that scores clarity of aims, adequacy of design, reproducibility of methods, and alignment of conclusions with results as separate items
•	Require a completed reporting guideline checklist at submission (CONSORT, STROBE, PRISMA, CARE, ARRIVE, SRQR/COREQ as applicable) and verify it against the manuscript during review
•	Separate statistical review into its own step and assign a statistical reviewer where warranted
•	Expand the reviewer pool to reduce reliance on internal reviewers and increase international representation
•	Do not waive the re-review step that confirms requested revisions were actually made
3.4 Pre-acceptance editorial gate
•	Make professional language editing a condition of acceptance
•	Run a data verification step that reconciles values across text, tables, figures, and abstract
•	Verify reference accuracy and link resolution mechanically, including DOI checks
•	Set a policy on citing theses and grey literature, and monitor a minimum share of recent peer-reviewed sources
•	Define an acceptable interval between data collection and publication, and require justification in the article when the interval is long
4. Working checklists
Checklist A. Submission and author stage
№	Item
A1	Is the study aim stated in the introduction in a single explicit sentence?
A2	Is the rationale for the chosen study design stated?
A3	Has the applicable reporting guideline checklist been submitted (CONSORT/STROBE/PRISMA/CARE, etc.)?
A4	Could an independent researcher reproduce the study from the methods alone (participants, sample size justification, instruments, procedure, statistics, software version)?
A5	Are the ethics committee, approval number, and consent procedure stated, and do they agree with the design described in the text?
A6	Does the abstract convey the content of the article on its own, in line with ICMJE Recommendations?
A7	Does every assertion carry a citation, and does each citation actually support the assertion?
A8	Does the reference list include sufficient recent peer-reviewed literature?
A9	Is reliance on theses and grey literature minimized, and do all links resolve?
A10	Is the interval between data collection and expected publication acceptable, with justification given if long?
A11	Do the conclusions stay within the scope of the results and answer the stated aim?
A12	Does the discussion state the limitations concretely?
A13	Are data presented once, without repetition between text and tables or figures?
A14	Is every abbreviation actually used more than once?
Checklist B. Peer review stage
№	Item
B1	Is this design adequate to answer the research question — and if not, is that grounds for rejection rather than revision?
B2	Can the study be reproduced from the methods, and if not, has the missing element been specified?
B3	Do the statistical methods fit the data and design, and are p values reported appropriately (e.g., p < 0.001)?
B4	Does anything appear in the discussion or conclusion that the results do not show?
B5	Does the generalization extend beyond the sample and setting studied?
B6	Does the discussion engage critically with conflicting prior work?
B7	For a review: does it comprehensively summarize and analyze recent research in the field rather than the authors' own work?
B8	Is each item of the reporting guideline checklist verifiable in the manuscript itself?
B9	Have 3–5 citations been sampled and checked against the assertions they support?
Checklist C. Editorial board, before acceptance
№	Item
C1	Have the requested revisions been verified against the revised manuscript?
C2	Do all values agree across text, abstract, tables, and figures?
C3	Does each table or figure cited in the text actually show the indicated data?
C4	Does every table and figure carry an informative, self-contained legend and annotation?
C5	Has professional copyediting resolved grammar, typographical, and logical flow problems?
C6	Have reference details and DOIs/URLs been verified mechanically?
C7	Where an editor or board member is an author, has the conflict-of-interest procedure been applied and disclosed?
C8	Does the article fall within the journal's stated aims and scope?
Checklist D. Journal level, reviewed annually
№	Item
D1	Is the proportion of original research consistent with the journal's stated aims?
D2	Is the proportion of articles authored by editors and board members calculated and kept within the internal ceiling?
D3	Are the size, institutional diversity, and international share of the reviewer pool increasing?
D4	Are aims and scope, peer review process, conflict-of-interest policy, and ethics standards published and current on the website?
D5	Does the editorial board listing include institutional affiliation and area of expertise for each member?
D6	Has the stated publication schedule and volume been met?
D7	Would the most recent 25 articles pass checklists A–C if audited internally?
D8	Is variability under control — would the weakest article in the most recent issue survive external review?
5. Order of implementation
With limited editorial resources, the following sequence is the most efficient.
1.	Eliminate what is immediately controllable: statistical reporting errors, typos and grammar, duplication between text and tables, missing legends, unnecessary abbreviations, reference errors, and broken links. These were raised in seven of nine letters yet are resolved by process alone.
2.	Introduce a structured review form and mandatory reporting guideline checklists so that irreproducible methods and overstated conclusions are caught during review.
3.	Treat adequacy of study design as a decision criterion. A manuscript whose design cannot answer its question should be rejected rather than revised; this is what reduces variability across the journal.
4.	Manage in-house authorship and article type mix as quantitative indicators with stated targets.
5.	Before the 24-month reapplication window opens, run an internal mock review of the most recent 25 articles and document the resulting record of improvement.
